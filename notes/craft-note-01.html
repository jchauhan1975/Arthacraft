Craft Notes / No. 01 / Governance

The model is not the product.

Benefits, risks and what not to do when you put an LLM inside something a regulated buyer will pay for.

Jitander ChauhanOctober 20269 min read

The question I got

Someone asked me last week: how do you actually use an LLM in a commercial product?

Not a demo. Not a chatbot bolted onto a website. A product a regulated buyer will pay for, audit and depend on.

It made me stop and think. Based on what we have actually delivered, what is the honest answer?

The model is not the product. The system around the model is.

Looking back at what we built and shipped, every hard lesson pointed the same way. The LLM is one component inside a deterministic, governed system. It is powerful, replaceable and untrustworthy on its own.

Treat it like a database or a payment gateway. Wrap it in contracts. Verify what it returns. Put a human where the decision matters.

Here is what that buys you, what it costs you, and the mistakes I would not make again.

What you gain when the model is governed

Built this way, an LLM product earns things a raw model never will.

Consistency you can defend

Give every step an explicit output contract. Tell the model exactly what shape to return and require evidence for each claim. When one provider added schema enforcement, adherence on its complex-schema tests went from under 40 percent to 100 percent. [1] That fixes the shape of an answer, not its truth. A perfectly formatted answer can still be wrong, which is why the contract also demands evidence.

Trust from regulated buyers

Banks and insurers do not buy cleverness. They buy control. Human approval gates, end-to-end traceability and single-tenant isolation are what get you through procurement. Regulation is moving the same way: the EU AI Act requires high-risk AI systems to be overseen by people who can override or stop them. [2]

Costs you can control

Route routine work to a mid-tier model. Reserve the top-tier model for high-stakes reasoning. [3] Cache stable prompts. [4] In our cost model, inference came out at roughly 1 to 10 percent of cost-to-serve. [5] The price of a given level of capability keeps falling, by 10x or more a year. [6] [7] Volume is the catch. Agents and reasoning models use far more tokens per task, and Gartner expects inference cost per agentic workflow to rise more than fivefold through 2028. [8] Watch volume, not just price.

No hostage situation

Route across more than one provider. Outages, price changes and policy shifts become operational events, not existential ones. Providers also retire models on a schedule, so a single-model product carries a built-in migration date. [9]

A moat that survives the next model

Your competitors can call the same API tomorrow. They cannot copy your workflow, your governance and your accumulated delivery knowledge overnight.

If a better model makes your product obsolete, you built a wrapper. If it makes your product better, you built a system.

The risks nobody puts in the pitch deck

They fall into six groups. Each is manageable. None is optional.

Cost

Humans are your biggest cost. In our model, expert review was about 56 percent of cost-to-serve and infrastructure about 4 percent. [5] Human-in-the-loop is the feature regulated buyers want, and the ceiling on your margin. Token volume is the other exposure: an agent stuck in a loop or an abusive user can burn a month of budget overnight, which is why unbounded consumption is on the industry's top-ten list of LLM risks. [10]

Data

Every call sends data to someone else. With a standard API key, prompts and outputs go to the provider, and what they keep is set by their terms. One major provider keeps API data for abuse monitoring for up to 30 days by default and offers zero retention only to approved customers. [11] Another offers zero retention per organization, with listed exclusions. [12] The key itself is a credential: leaked, it exposes data and runs up your bill. Ask early whether a buyer requires models to run through its own cloud account. For some, that decides the deal.

Security

Treat the model as an untrusted party. Instructions hidden in any document it reads can steer its output. [13] Passing that output straight into SQL, a shell or a web page is a classic injection hole. Agents with broad write, send or spend permissions turn one bad output into an incident, and anything in a system prompt should be assumed extractable. [10] Our own version was quieter: one stale design file, carrying content from an unrelated system, was enough to send coding agents toward the wrong primitives.

Reliability

The same input does not always give the same answer, even at temperature zero; one root cause is how servers batch requests under load. [14] Models behind moving aliases change without a code change, providers retire versions on a schedule, [9] and long inputs degrade: models use information at the start and end of a long context better than the middle. [15] Plan for rate limits, timeouts and outages as you would for any other dependency.

Quality

Output can be confidently wrong. We once ran an AI-assisted gap analysis of our own codebase, and about 40 percent of its findings were wrong once checked against the code. [5] This is not unusual. In a study of 576,000 code samples, at least 5.2 percent of packages suggested by commercial models, and 21.7 percent by open-source ones, did not exist. [16] A 2026 re-test of five frontier models still found 4.6 to 6.1 percent. [17] NIST names the pattern confabulation: confidently stated but false content. [18] And prompting is the easy part. In our build, writing a prompt took days; wiring it into the engine, with context, parsing and failure handling, took far longer.

Compliance

Not a later phase. US banking regulators state that using a third party does not reduce a bank's responsibility, and expect due diligence on a vendor's security controls, including SOC reports where available. [19] [20] No regulation names SOC 2, but it is what procurement will ask for. Budget for audit logs, MFA, SSO and that report from day one. Decide too what you show: prompts, methods and recorded demos all expose how you work.

What to do

Seven practices cover all six groups of risk.

  1. Write a contract for every call, and validate against it.Shape, count, naming and required evidence. Check every response before anything downstream sees it.
  2. Treat model output as untrusted input.Escape it, parameterize it and validate it like anything a stranger typed. Never execute it directly.
  3. Give agents least privilege, with a human gate.Pre-approve a short list of safe actions. Everything else goes to a person, and the approval lives in your system of record, never in the tool doing the work. [2]
  4. Vault the keys and cap the volume.Keys per environment, rotated, never in code. Token and job limits per tier. Choose the data path, including retention terms and zero retention where needed, before the first client document goes through.
  5. Pin model versions and test every change.Use dated versions, not aliases that move. Run a fixed set of real cases before switching, and change the model or the prompt, never both at once.
  6. Log every call.Input, context version, prompt version, model version, output, reviewer and decision. If you cannot replay an answer, you cannot defend it.
  7. Plan for failure.Retries with backoff, timeouts, a fallback model or provider, and a mode where work queues for a person instead of failing.

What not to do

Five mistakes we made, nearly made, or caught just in time.

  1. Do not ship the model as the product.A prompt and a UI is a feature someone else will ship next quarter. Build the workflow the model sits inside.
  2. Do not accept a negative claim without proof.If the model says something is missing, require a search or citation that shows it. Label every finding as verified, inferred, assumed or unknown.
  3. Do not control cost by removing features.Feature-gating does not cap spend. Capping token and job volume does. Strip features and you strip the reason customers pay.
  4. Do not let agents work from unverified context.Audit what agents read. Work from live project files, not stale summaries of them.
  5. Do not let the AI approve its own work.The human holds the decisions. The agent holds the keyboard.

Build it deliberately

LLMs are the most capable component most of us have ever integrated.

They are also the least predictable.

The teams that win commercially will not be the ones with the best prompts. They will be the ones who treat the model as a governed component: contracted, verified, observed and overseen by a human where it matters.

Speed comes from the model. Trust comes from the system.

Build the system.

Figures marked [5] come from our internal cost-to-serve model and review records, not audited production data. Treat them as directional.
